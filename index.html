01 · Healer & Learner

A continuum, not a straight line.

Medicine, public health, and data science aren't three separate careers — they're one line of inquiry, followed wherever it led.

I began as a physician, trained for clinical excellence and service — but the patients I treated kept pointing me toward questions medicine alone couldn't answer.

20+ yrsClinical practice — emergency, ENT, GI & general medicine
MPH → MSPublic Health, then Data Science (Health Analytics)

More than two decades of clinical work — across emergency care, otorhinolaryngology, gastroenterology, and general practice — gave me a close view of what patients actually face: delayed prevention, limited access, socioeconomic barriers, and gaps in health information that no prescription can fix.

That observation pulled me from treating individuals toward understanding populations. A Master of Public Health took me into community health initiatives, chronic disease programs, and surveillance-informed decision-making — and showed me how directly good data shapes policy and outcomes, especially in resource-limited settings.

As my interests sharpened around cancer epidemiology, chronic disease, and health equity, I realized I needed a new set of tools — so I added a Master of Science in Data Science (Health Analytics) to the foundation, working with large datasets like NHANES, and the All of Us Research Program, and building fluency in R, SAS, Python, SQL, and GIS.

Read the fuller journey

My path has also crossed countries and health systems. Navigating extended transitions as a single working mother during my spouse's doctoral training in the United States built a resilience and cultural perspective that never interrupted my academic growth — if anything, it deepened my understanding of health disparities and sharpened my commitment to academic medicine and public health.

Today my data science work stays grounded in clinical and public-health relevance. I don't see analytics as a purely computational exercise — it's a way to identify risk patterns, sharpen prevention strategies, and make evidence legible to researchers, policymakers, and communities alike. My current research centers on cancer epidemiology: the intersection of chronic disease, metabolic risk, mental health, and health inequities, including work with breast cancer cohorts from the All of Us Research Program.

I'm now looking for an academic home where clinical experience, public health training, and data science expertise are valued together — through teaching, collaborative research, mentorship, and service, ideally at an institution committed to health equity and evidence-driven care.
